# Factorising Quadratics

Factorising is the reverse of expanding. You start with a quadratic like
x squared + 5x + 6 and rewrite it as a product of two brackets.

## The standard form

A quadratic is written as x squared + bx + c. To factorise it into
(x + p)(x + q), you need two numbers that:
- multiply to give c (the constant term)
- add to give b (the coefficient of x)

## A worked example

Factorise x squared + 5x + 6.
- We need two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 5.
- The pairs that multiply to 6 are: 1 and 6, 2 and 3.
- The pair 2 and 3 adds to 5. That is the one.
- So x squared + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3).

## When the middle term is negative

Factorise x squared - 5x + 6.
- Two numbers that multiply to +6 and add to -5.
- Since they multiply to a positive but add to a negative, both are negative.
- -2 and -3 multiply to 6 and add to -5.
- So x squared - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3).

## Using factorising to solve equations

If (x - 2)(x - 3) = 0, then one of the brackets must equal zero, because the
only way two numbers multiply to zero is if at least one of them is zero.
- Either x - 2 = 0, giving x = 2
- Or x - 3 = 0, giving x = 3
So the solutions are x = 2 or x = 3.

## Checking

Expand your brackets back out. If you get the original quadratic, your
factorising is correct.
